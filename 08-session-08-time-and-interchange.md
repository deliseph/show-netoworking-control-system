## Timecode

Timecode is an agreement about what moment it is, expressed as **hours, minutes, seconds and frames**,
attached to a piece of media or generated by a machine, so that several systems can run against the
same moment without any of them controlling the others.

`01:23:45:12` is one hour, twenty three minutes, forty five seconds and twelve frames.

That last field is the one that causes all the trouble, because **a frame is not a fixed length of
time.** It depends on the frame rate, and the industry uses several.

### Frame rates, and the one that is not a whole number

| Rate | Where it comes from | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 24 | Film | Cinema, and anything mastered from film |
| 25 | 50 Hz mains, PAL/SECAM territories | **Europe, Hong Kong, most of Asia, Australia. The default here.** |
| 29.97 | NTSC colour, and the reason drop frame exists | North America, Japan, broadcast |
| 30 | NTSC monochrome, and "30 non-drop" | US audio and some show control |
| 50, 59.94, 60 | High frame rate video | Sport, LED walls, modern video |

29.97 is not a rounding of 30. When colour was added to NTSC in 1953, the frame rate was pulled down
by a factor of exactly 1000/1001 to fit the colour subcarrier in without interfering with the sound.
So the real rate is 30 × 1000 ÷ 1001 = **29.97002997…** frames per second.

### Drop frame, which drops no frames

<!--anim:dropframe-->

Here is the problem that creates. If you count 30 frames per second while the frames are actually
arriving at 29.97 per second, your timecode clock runs **slow relative to real time**: it falls
behind by about 3.6 seconds every hour.

For an hour-long broadcast programme that is unacceptable, so **drop-frame timecode** was invented.
The rule:

> Skip frame numbers **00** and **01** at the start of every minute, **except** at minutes divisible
> by ten.

That is 2 frames skipped per minute, 18 minutes out of 20 in each ten-minute group, so 108 frames
skipped per hour. At 29.97 fps, 108 frames is 3.6036 seconds, which is almost exactly the error. Over
an hour the residual is a couple of milliseconds.

**Nothing is dropped from the recording.** The frames all exist. Only the *numbers* are skipped, the
way a building skips a thirteenth floor. Drop frame is written with semicolons — `01:23:45;12` — or
with a `;` before the frames field, and non-drop with colons throughout.

Two operational consequences:

- **Non-drop 29.97 timecode is a valid choice** and is common in audio work, because a music session
  does not care about matching a wall clock. It is only wrong when it has to match a broadcast clock.
- **A drop-frame and a non-drop source will drift apart by 3.6 seconds an hour** while both appear
  entirely healthy. This is a real fault and it presents as "the video is slipping" late in a long
  show.

**In 25 fps territories, none of this exists.** 25 is exactly 25, there is no drop frame at 25 fps,
and if somebody offers you drop frame at 25 they have misconfigured something.

### LTC: timecode as an audio signal

<!--anim:ltc-waveform-->

**Linear Timecode**, SMPTE 12M, encodes 80 bits per frame as an audio-frequency square wave, using
**biphase mark encoding**: there is a transition at every bit boundary, and an extra transition in
the middle of the bit if it is a one.

That encoding is chosen for three properties, all of which matter in a venue:

- **It is self-clocking.** The transitions carry the clock, so no separate clock line is needed.
- **It is polarity independent.** Swap the two conductors and it still decodes, which matters on a
  patch bay.
- **It works at any speed**, forwards or backwards, because the decoder reads transitions rather than
  sampling at a fixed rate. This is why a machine shuttling can still read timecode.

Practically: LTC is an audio signal, at about −10 to 0 dBu, on a standard audio cable. It can go
anywhere audio goes, including down a channel of a multitrack, a radio link, or an audio-over-IP
network. That is enormously convenient and it is why LTC is still ubiquitous.

Its limits: at 80 bits per frame at 25 fps that is 2000 bit/s, so the audio bandwidth needed is small,
and it cannot be read when stopped, because there are no transitions. A machine parked on a frame
outputs nothing.

**VITC**, vertical interval timecode, solves that by putting the code in the invisible lines of a
video signal, so it can be read when paused. In a world of file-based video it is much less common
than it was, and you will meet it mostly in broadcast.

### MIDI Time Code

<!--anim:mtc-quarter-->

**MTC** carries SMPTE timecode over MIDI. Its design is constrained by MIDI's 31.25 kbit/s, and the
solution is characteristically economical:

A full timecode value is eight nibbles: hours, minutes, seconds, frames, each split into two 4-bit
pieces. MTC sends **one nibble every quarter frame**, as a two-byte message `F1 <data>`, so a
complete timecode value takes **eight quarter-frame messages, which is two frames**.

The consequences:

- **MTC is inherently two frames behind**, and a receiver must account for that. Good implementations
  do; the offset is why an MTC-driven cue can sit slightly late against an LTC-driven one.
- **The direction is encoded in the order**, so MTC works running backwards.
- **A full-frame message** exists, sent as a SysEx, for locating: it delivers a complete timecode
  value at once when the machine jumps rather than runs.

MTC is the right choice when the two devices already have a MIDI link and the accuracy needed is a
frame or two. It is the wrong choice for anything sample-accurate, which is what PTP and word clock
are for.

### The other ways to make things happen together

<!--anim:sync-methods-->

Timecode is one of five, and choosing wrongly is common.

| Method | Accuracy | What it is for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Timecode, LTC or MTC | Frame | Running several systems against fixed media | Drop frame mismatches; MTC's two-frame offset |
| PTP, IEEE 1588 | Sub-microsecond | Media over IP: Dante, AES67, ST 2110 | One grandmaster, one profile, one VLAN |
| Genlock / black burst / tri-level | Sub-line | Video systems sharing a scan | Legacy, but still the backbone of a broadcast plant |
| Word clock, AES3 | Sample | Digital audio between boxes | Being replaced by PTP |
| Network cues, OSC or MSC | 5 to 50 ms | Discrete moments, not continuous running | Not a clock; it is an event |
| Ableton Link | Musical beat | Musicians on a shared network tempo | Beat, not time. Different thing. |

**The distinction that matters.** Timecode and PTP are *clocks*: they answer "what time is it now?"
continuously. MSC and OSC are *events*: they say "do this thing". A great many system design mistakes
are using an event where a clock was needed, which shows as things that start together and drift, or
using a clock where an event was needed, which shows as an inability to say "not yet".

## OSC, properly

**Open Sound Control** came out of CNMAT at Berkeley in 1997, as a replacement for MIDI for musical
control. It became the general-purpose control protocol of this industry almost by accident.

<!--anim:osc-message-->

An OSC message has three parts:

```
/eos/chan/1/level    ,f    75.0
└── address ──────┘  └tag┘ └arg┘
```

1. **An address pattern**, a string beginning with `/`, structured like a filesystem path. It is
   hierarchical on purpose, and it supports wildcards: `/light/*/level` addresses all of them.
2. **A type tag string**, beginning with `,`, saying what the arguments are.
3. **The arguments**, in that order.

| Tag | Type | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `i` | 32-bit integer | |
| `f` | 32-bit float | The one you use most: levels, positions, times |
| `s` | String | Null-terminated, padded to a multiple of 4 |
| `b` | Blob | Arbitrary binary |
| `T` `F` | True, False | No argument bytes at all |
| `N` | Nil | |
| `t` | Time tag | 64-bit NTP format |
| `h` `d` | 64-bit integer, double | OSC 1.1 |

Everything is padded to a multiple of four bytes, which is why OSC messages are bigger than they look
and why a hand-built one that does not work is usually a padding error.

### Bundles and time tags

<!--anim:osc-bundle-->

An OSC **bundle** is `#bundle`, a 64-bit time tag, then several messages. Everything in a bundle is
to be executed **atomically**, at the moment the time tag names.

That is a genuinely useful feature and it is the one people forget OSC has. It means you can send
"set these twelve things, all at exactly 20:15:03.500" and have it happen together rather than in the
order the packets arrived.

The time tag is in NTP format: seconds since 1900 plus a fraction. The special value `1` means
"immediately". **Most implementations only support immediately.** Check before designing around
scheduled bundles; QLab and a few others honour them properly and a great many devices do not.

### The dialect problem

<!--anim:osc-dialects-->

OSC specifies how to encode a message. It says nothing about what the addresses should be, and there
is no registry, no discovery and no assigned port.

So:

| System | Example address | Port |
| --- | --- | --- |
| QLab | `/cue/12.5/start` | 53000 |
| ETC Eos | `/eos/cue/1/5/fire` | 3032 |
| grandMA3 | `/gma3/cmd` | 8000 by configuration |
| Resolume | `/composition/layers/1/clips/1/connect` | 7000 |
| disguise | `/d3/showcontrol/play` | 7401 |
| TouchOSC | Whatever you design | Whatever you choose |

None of those are wrong and none of them are compatible. **Every OSC integration is bespoke**, and
the deliverable for an OSC integration is a documented list of the addresses actually used, kept with
the show file.

That is the real cost of OSC's flexibility, and it is worth stating in a production meeting when
somebody says "it speaks OSC so it will just work".

### OSC over UDP or TCP

OSC has no assigned transport either. UDP is the norm and is right for anything timed. TCP is
available in most implementations and is right when the message must not be lost. Where TCP is used,
messages are usually length-prefixed or SLIP-framed, and **the two framings are not compatible**,
which is a real interoperability trap.

## The rest of the landscape

This is the survey section. You will not use all of these, and you will meet each of them eventually,
and the aim is that none of them is a surprise.

### Device control and monitoring

- **AES70 / OCA.** An object-based control architecture for audio devices, standardised by AES,
  currently at AES70-2023. Technically thorough, genuinely open, and adoption remains modest: most
  audio manufacturers still ship their own control protocol. Worth knowing exists and worth checking
  whether the devices you specified support it, because when they do it is much nicer than the
  alternative.
- **SNMP.** Simple Network Management Protocol, on UDP 161 with traps on 162. The standard way to
  monitor network equipment, and increasingly present on show devices: switches, UPS units,
  amplifiers, projectors. A monitoring system that polls SNMP and alerts on a fault is a genuinely
  cheap way to know a rack is overheating before the show does.
- **VNC and remote desktop.** How a media server or a show computer gets driven from front of house.
  Port 5900 for VNC. It is a screen-sharing protocol, not a control protocol: it is bandwidth-hungry,
  it has no concept of a cue, and it should never be the path a cue takes. Fine for a technician,
  wrong for a show.
- **HTTP, WebSocket and MQTT.** The general-purpose APIs. MQTT deserves a mention because it is
  appearing in themed entertainment and installations: a lightweight publish-and-subscribe protocol
  over TCP, designed for many small devices reporting to a broker, and increasingly how sensors and
  props report state on a large site.

### Positional tracking

<!--anim:psn-tracking-->

Following a performer with a light, a camera or a sound image requires knowing where they are, and
this has become a mature product category.

| System | Technology | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BlackTrax | Active infrared LED beacons and camera array | The established one in touring and theatre |
| Zactrak | UWB radio, plus IR | No line of sight needed, which is the big practical difference |
| Pozyx, Ubisense | UWB | Industrial roots, used in installations |
| Optical mocap, camera-based | Passive markers or markerless | Broadcast and virtual production |

The interchange format is what matters to you: **PosiStageNet, PSN.** It is an open, free protocol
published by VYV and MA Lighting that carries tracker positions over UDP multicast, and it is the
common language between tracking systems and the consoles, media servers and audio processors that
consume them. If a tracking system and a console both speak PSN, they interoperate. If not, you are
buying a manufacturer's bridge.

### Industrial control, and where it meets a show

<!--anim:industrial-buses-->

Themed entertainment, large installations and anything with machinery in it will put you next to
industrial automation, and its vocabulary is different.

| Protocol | What it is | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Modbus RTU / TCP | Very simple register read and write, 1979 vintage, still everywhere | Building systems, HVAC, simple machinery, sensors |
| CANopen | Deterministic bus for motion, from automotive | Motors, drives, animatronics |
| EtherCAT | Very fast deterministic Ethernet-based fieldbus | Motion control, machinery, anything with a servo |
| PROFINET / PROFIBUS | Siemens ecosystem industrial networking | Large installations, European machinery |
| OPC UA | Vendor-neutral industrial data model over TCP | Supervisory systems, integration between PLCs and IT |
| BACnet | Building automation | HVAC, house lights, blinds, building integration |
| DALI-2 / D4i | Addressable architectural lighting | Front of house, foyers, architectural fixtures |
| KNX | Building control | European installations, house lighting |

The one to actually learn if you go into themed entertainment is **Modbus TCP**, because it is
trivially simple, absolutely everywhere, and it is often how a show controller reads a limit switch
or writes a setpoint.

### Machinery, safety and why it is a separate discipline

<!--anim:safety-levels-->

Session 2 said that safety functions do not go on the show network. Here is the fuller picture,
because you will sit in meetings where this is discussed.

Machinery that can hurt somebody is designed to a **performance level** under EN ISO 13849 or a
**safety integrity level** under IEC 62061. Those levels are calculated from the probability of a
dangerous failure, and they demand specific architectures: dual channels, cross-monitoring, and the
detection of a single fault before a second one can accumulate.

For entertainment specifically:

- **EN 17206** is the current European standard for machinery for stages and other production areas.
  It will be cited on any tender for powered flying, automation, lifts or revolves in Europe.
- **ANSI E1.6** is the ESTA series for powered hoist systems, and **E1.43** covers performer flying.
- A **safety bus** — PROFIsafe, Safety over EtherCAT, CIP Safety, CANopen Safety — carries safety
  signals over an industrial network with the diagnostic coverage the standards require. It is
  certified as a system, it is not a protocol you configure, and it is not on the show network.

**What this means for you as a networking person:** the machinery contractor's safety system is not
yours to design, integrate or troubleshoot. What *is* yours is the boundary: the show network may
send a machinery system a cue, and it may read status back for display, and neither of those paths
may be capable of causing motion that a safety system would not permit. Draw that boundary on the
system drawing explicitly. It is one of the most valuable lines on it.

### Media over IP, which is now most of the traffic

<!--anim:media-over-ip-->

The single biggest change since the standard text: audio and video now travel on the same network
as control, and they dominate its load.

| Technology | Layer | Latency | Clock | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dante** | Layer 3, IP | 0.25 to 5 ms selectable | PTPv2 | Proprietary, licensed, overwhelmingly dominant in live audio |
| **AES67** | Layer 3, IP | 0.25 to 5 ms | PTPv2, media profile | The open interoperability standard. Dante devices can speak it. |
| **Milan / AVB** | Layer 2, 802.1BA | Very low, reserved | gPTP, 802.1AS | Genuinely deterministic; needs AVB switches end to end |
| **SMPTE ST 2110** | Layer 3, IP | Sub-frame | PTPv2, ST 2059-2 | Separate essence streams for video, audio and data. Broadcast, large venues. |
| **NDI** | Layer 3, IP | 1 to 3 frames | None required | Compressed, works on ordinary networks, extremely convenient |
| **SRT** | Layer 3, IP | Configurable, hundreds of ms | None | Reliable transport over unreliable links; contribution and remote |
| **HDBaseT** | Point to point over Cat cable | Sub-frame | None | Not a network. A cable extension technology that uses the same connector. |

Two points that catch people out:

**HDBaseT is not networking.** It uses RJ45 connectors and Cat cable and it is a point-to-point
extension of HDMI. Plugging it into a switch does not work, and it must be a dedicated cable run.
This confuses people every single year.

**AES67 is a compatibility mode, not a product.** A Dante device in AES67 mode can exchange audio
with an AES67 device, with restrictions: usually 48 kHz, usually 1 ms latency, and often only a
subset of channels. It is genuinely useful and it is not the same as full Dante interoperability.

### The last few

- **CEC**, Consumer Electronics Control, over HDMI. It can power a display on and switch its input.
  It is unreliable between manufacturers and it is not a show control path, but for a simple
  installation with one display it can save a relay.
- **Bluetooth LE.** Increasingly how a fixture, a wireless DMX unit or a sensor is configured from a
  phone. Short range, no place in a cue path, and a genuine convenience during a fit-up.
- **UWB.** Ultra-wideband, centimetre-accurate ranging, the technology under several tracking systems.
- **Sony 9-pin, RS-422 machine control.** Obsolete and worth recognising: a nine-pin D-sub controlling
  a tape machine's transport. If you meet one you are in an old facility or a museum, and the modern
  equivalent is MMC or a manufacturer's API.

## Putting a system together

<!--anim:system-drawing-->

The final exercise of the module is to design a system and draw it. Here is what a good one contains,
and it is the same list whether the show is a studio piece or an arena tour.

**One page, and it fits.** If it needs three pages it needs to be three drawings at different levels:
an overview, a network detail, a control detail.

**Every box is named the way it is labelled in the venue.** A drawing whose names do not match the
labels on the racks is worse than no drawing.

**Every line says what protocol it is**, and its physical medium if that is not obvious. `sACN over
fibre`, `MSC over RTP-MIDI`, `contact closure`, `Dante primary`, `Dante secondary`. This is the single
highest-value thing on the page, and it is the thing most drawings omit.

**The singular roles are named.** DHCP server. IGMP querier. PTP grandmaster. Show control master.
Three of those are the things nobody can find at 22:00.

**The boundaries are drawn.** Where the show network stops and the house network starts. Where the
machinery contractor's safety system begins. Where the venue's responsibility ends and yours begins.

**A failure table.** Every critical path, what happens if it fails, and what the fallback is. This is
the Five Questions applied to the system rather than to a protocol, and it is the document a
production manager will actually read.

| Path | If it fails | Fallback | Who acts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Console to lighting network | Fixtures hold last look | Backup console at sACN priority 90 | Nobody, it is automatic |
| Show control to playback | The cue never happens | Operator fires locally from the machine | Sound number two |
| PTP grandmaster | Audio drifts, then mutes | Backup grandmaster takes over in seconds | Nobody, if configured |
| Timecode | Video freewheels, then stops | Manual GO from the stage manager | Stage manager, on cue |

Filling that table in for a real production is a genuinely professional piece of work, and very few
people do it.

## Extension: what to be suspicious of

A short list of claims you will hear, and what to ask.

- **"It's all on the network so it's flexible."** Ask which VLAN, and what happens when the switch
  reboots.
- **"It supports OSC."** Ask for the address list, and whether it is documented or discovered.
- **"It's a standard, so it will interoperate."** Ask which parts are implemented, and whether anyone
  has actually tested these two products together.
- **"We'll put the E-stop on the network."** Say no, and explain why in one sentence.
- **"Wireless is fine now."** Ask what happens when two thousand phones arrive.
- **"We can add that later."** Ask for the address space, the switch ports and the universes now,
  because those are the three things that are painful to retrofit.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Drop-frame timecode drops frames."** It skips frame *numbers*, two at the start of each minute
  except every tenth minute, so the count matches real time at 29.97 fps. Every frame of the
  recording is present.
- **"29.97 is just 30 rounded."** It is 30 × 1000 ÷ 1001 exactly, a consequence of fitting the colour
  subcarrier into NTSC in 1953. The difference is 3.6 seconds an hour, which is why drop frame exists.
- **"We use drop frame at 25 fps for accuracy."** There is no drop frame at 25 fps. 25 is exactly 25.
  A device offering it has been misconfigured.
- **"MTC is timecode, so it is frame accurate."** MTC delivers one nibble per quarter frame, so a
  complete value takes two frames and MTC is inherently two frames behind. A good implementation
  compensates; not all do.
- **"OSC will just work between the two systems."** OSC defines an encoding, not a vocabulary. Every
  product invents its own addresses and picks its own port, so every integration is bespoke and its
  deliverable is a documented address list kept with the show file.
- **"An OSC bundle schedules things for later."** It can, and most implementations only support the
  time tag meaning "immediately". Test it on the actual devices before designing around it.
- **"HDBaseT is a network protocol because it uses Cat cable."** It is a point-to-point cable
  extension for HDMI. It does not go through a switch, and it needs its own dedicated run.
- **"AES67 mode means Dante and AES67 devices are fully interoperable."** It means they can exchange
  audio under restrictions: usually 48 kHz, usually 1 ms, often a subset of channels, and generally
  without Dante's own discovery and control. Useful, and not the same thing.
- **"Safety systems are just another protocol on the network."** They are certified systems designed
  to a performance level or safety integrity level, with dual channels and fault detection, on a
  rated safety bus or hardwired. They are not on the show network and they are not yours to design.
